Two main governance-related factors that have a detrimental effect on members in an OSSD community are a lack of
process transparency and of consent amongst members on decisions made in the community.3 In particular, researchers
have noted that lack of transparency in the decision-making process can alienate members and erode the sense of

    There is a lack of empirical research that explores OSS decision-making processes.5,6 Some OSS projects specifically
make their decision-making process explicit, while others do not.7 Even when the process details are explicitly made avail-
able, the level of detail available within a process can be problematic. Prior work has shown that the high-level decision
processes made available in the Python project suffer from a lack of detail8 thus rendering them less effective for some
stakeholders. So, the challenge with unknown or incomplete processes is that the contributors may not have any under-
standing or possess only a superficial understanding of one of the key aspects of OSS governance, the decision-making
processes.
    Duffy et al.9 explain this by presenting an example of new members getting involved in OSS projects that use a
meritocracy-based governance approach. She notes that since there are no job titles, organizational charts, or design
consultancy, it is initially hard for new people to identify the appropriate members who can help them to carry out
their tasks. Duffy further explains that members can only achieve this after exercising “patience and careful observa-
tion to fully understand the community’s structure, and hierarchy”9p. 2 . In addition, even for an internal stakeholder
who has been well-integrated within a group, the decision-making procedures may not be readily available in a sin-
gle document. One might have to sift through several sources of information (e.g., wikis, project web pages, FAQs, and
mailing list discussions) and integrate them to understand how decisions are made, which can be both tedious and
time consuming.10,11 Moreover, even after piecing together different aspects of the process from various documents, the
overall process may still be incomplete.8 Additionally, the processes that are enacted may be different to the advertised
processes.7
    This work aims to address the above-mentioned gaps by discovering the enacted decision-making processes based
on data available in OSS repositories. The Python project has been chosen because of its widespread usage and popu-
larity;12 its reputation of following good governance practices;13 the research interest it has garnered;7,8 and because it
makes all its data available to the public (e.g., in the form of email archives and commit repositories7,14,15 ). Additionally,
Python operates based on a mixed governance model including elements of both meritocracy and autocracy.16 Seldom
has prior work focused on decision-making of such a mixed governance approach. Also, even when decision models have
been studied there has only been a qualitative focus, where high-level models are presented based on interviews and
surveys with stakeholders. For example, the work of Mrowka,4 presents the decision-making model of the Apache com-
munity, which employs a meritocracy approach. Mrowka’s work does not extract the enacted model using a data-driven,
bottom-up approach as we undertake in this work. Another reason for studying DM processes in a mixed DM model is
that the analysis would highlight the spectrum of DM processes compared to focusing on a project that uses only one DM
model.
    To this end, in this study we explore methods for mining DM processes from the email archives of OSS projects
(the Python developer mailing lists) so that insights of the DM process can be revealed. This work is an extension of
our initial work reported in Sharma et al.16 by presenting more extensive results, with 80% new material. The basis
for both our works is to scrutinize the Python decision-making process reported in the literature—those contained
within the Figures A1–A3, which are attached in Appendix. A more detailed Python decision-making process is pre-
sented using four new tables and five new figures, highlighting aspects not reported earlier. In addition, we further
break down RQ2 into sub-parts while the methodology is much more detailed (11 steps in this paper compared to
four steps in16 ).
    The main objective of our work is being able to automatically extract the overall DM process in OSSD unearthing the
various activities that take place that are currently hidden in email archives. In doing so, we propose a purposely-built
framework called Decision-Making Process (DeMaP) Miner, which can be used to extract DM processes from OSS reposi-
tories with similar structure. Thus, this paper makes two key contributions: (i) a methodological contribution in the form
of a framework that can extract DM processes from Python repositories, which can be applied to other similar OSS repos-
itories; and (ii) knowledge contributions through (a) extracting the actual DM process used in the Python community
and comparing this new process against previously described processes, and (b) highlighting the differences between DM
processes used for the three different PEP types.
    The article is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the background of the Python OSS project (our case study), con-
textualizes prior work in this arena, and subsequently presents the research questions. Section 3 details the approach used
to extract the Python DM process. Section 4 presents the results and Section 5 evaluates the utility of the extracted pro-
cess. Section 6 presents the generic DeMaP Miner framework for mining DM processes from OSSD repositories structured
similar to Python. Section 7 discusses contributions, limitations, and future work. Section 8 concludes this work.

2       BAC KG ROU N D AN D R E LATED WORK O N THE PYTHON OSS PROJ ECT

We examine OSS DM processes through the case study of Python, a large well-established OSS community. Within the
Python community enhancements to the language, that is, a new idea or feature, are made via a python enhancement
proposal (PEP). There are three types of PEPs:17

• Process PEPs describe the processes used in the Python community, for example, PEP 1 (PEP Purpose and Guidelines)
  documents the reviewing process used to decide the outcome of proposals.
• Informational PEPs elaborate on issues related to Python design or contain guidelines for members.
• Standards Track PEPs describe new proposals that enhance the language.

    These PEPs are shared with members through the developer mailing list (python-dev) and their opinions are solicited
and discussed. PEP 117 identifies eight main states a PEP document can pass through during the development process
(see Appendix Figure A1). Most PEPs end up in the Final, Rejected, or Withdrawn state, while some (e.g., Process PEPs)
stay in the Active state until revoked or superseded.
    There has been some prior work7,8 on extracting DM states and creating a process diagram of DM in Python. These
studies identified a discrepancy between the “as-is” process and the “should-be” process. Based on historical commit
messages for PEP documents, these prior works found five additional main states and showed that the actual process
(Figure A2) is more complex than the advertised process (Figure A1). However, the processes these studies extracted
are document-centric and state-based. That is, they only consider what happens to the PEP document artifacts by con-
sidering just their states (e.g., a document moves from Draft to Accepted to Final), without providing a comprehensive
overview of what led to certain decisions. For example, these document centric processes cannot answer questions such
as whether a decision was made based on consensus amongst developers, or by pronouncement from a leader despite
a lack of consensus. This gap can be bridged by analyzing email messages exchanged between Python core developers,
to provide valuable insights into DM activities that lead to state changes in PEPs. For example, analyzing these mes-
sages will reveal the underlying consensus building activities, such as voting, that caused a PEP to move from Draft to
Accepted. In other words, this analysis will highlight the unknown DM sub-states that occur between the already-known
main states.
    PEP 117 also includes a textual description of the roles of community members in each phase of PEP approval in
addition to the diagram shown in Figure A1.18 Based on this description we manually created a flowchart of the Python
DM process (Figure A3). The overall process was divided into five conceptual stages (highlighted in Figure A3 by dashed
red outlines): (i) Idea, where the proposal is still in its formative stages; (ii) Draft, where the proposal is formally drafted
after being deemed worth pursuing; (iii) Community Feedback, where initial comments towards a proposal are solicited
from the community; (iv) Review, where the proposal is reviewed by the core developers; and (v) Review-Update, where
appropriate amendments are made if needed.
    Figure A3 encapsulates all that is publicly known about the Python DM process. Similar to the finding of Savarimuthu
et al.,8 who identified additional states as shown in Figure A2, we contend that vital information about what leads to
proposals being accepted or rejected is not included in Figure A3. It is also not known whether Figure A3 accurately
depicts how PEP decisions are actually made. Given the limited prior work,7,8 one of our goals is to assess whether the
DM process shown in Figure A3 is indeed the actual process that is used to enact decisions in Python. To achieve this
goal, we need to extract the enacted DM process. Thus, our research objective is to design and develop an approach
that extracts the decision-making process based on the data available in email discussions. Meeting this research
objective enables the answering of the following two research questions (RQs):

RQ1. What insights can be obtained from the newly extracted decision-making process when compared to
     previously advertised processes (i.e., those shown in Figures A1–A3)?.
RQ2. What are the similarities and differences between the process models for the three PEP types (Process,
     Informational, and Standards Track) when compared to:
       a. each other
       b. the overall DM process model for all PEP types, and
       c. the DM process models reported in the literature?

3           M ET H O D O LO GY

Our work adopts design science research (DSR), which is commonly used in Software Engineering and Information Sys-
tems research.19,20 DSR is most-suited for a problem-solving paradigm that focuses on designing a solution to a problem.21
DSR research can focus on producing artifacts (e.g., software), theories, or both.22 Our objective here is to develop
a software artifact, that is, operationalizing a framework in the form of software that can be used to extract enacted
processes.
    To create the software framework called Decision Making Process miner (DeMaP Miner for short), we followed the
design science research methodology (DSRM).23 The steps involved: (a) defining the objective of the software, which
is to extract enacted processes; (b) developing the software; and (c) evaluating the accuracy and utility of the processes
extracted by the software by (i) comparing against manually curated ground truth data, and (ii) seeking feedback from
one of the Python steering council members. The steps involved in the construction and the evaluation of the DeMaP
Miner software* are presented next.
    The methodology for extracting the DM process model for PEPs comprised 11 steps, which are described in
detail below and outlined in Figure 1. The dataset used in this study comprised data from three sources: (a) meta-
data about each PEP (see step 1 below), (b) state change data for each PEP (step 2), and (c) email discussions
drawn from various Python mailing lists (step 3). It spanned the period from the first email posted to the python-dev
mailing list (March 1995) through to the date the Benevolent Dictator for Life (BDFL), Guido van Rossum, resigned
from his post (11th July 2018, just after accepting PEP 572). This enabled us to capture all instances of DM car-
ried out in the Python project using a combination of the meritocracy-based DM principle and the dictatorship-based
DM principle.

Step 1: Collecting PEP metadata

The first step was to download all 463 PEPs from the above-mentioned period. Among other details, a PEP document
contains the PEP number, its authors’ details, and BDFL Delegates (if any) who made the final decision on the PEP. We
obtained the PEPs from Python’s Github repository† and stored the extracted metadata in a MySQL database.


Step 2: Collecting PEP state data

Next, we retrieved all the states (out of the 13 possible main states) that each of the aforementioned 463 PEPs transitioned
through.7 This information was extracted for all PEPs from the commit history in Python’s GitHub repository, which
includes all versions of every PEP document. For example, PEP 465 traversed through the states Draft, Accepted, and Final.


Step 3: Processing PEP emails

We then extracted from the Python email archives all individual email messages that fell within the above-mentioned
period, and stored them in a MySQL database. We also extracted all further email messages pertaining specifically to PEP
572 (the of which was posted on 30th October 2018) so that we had a complete record for all PEPs within the period.
We used the following email archives: python-dev, python-ideas, python-commits, python-checkins, python-distutils-sig,
python-3000, python-list, python-bugs-list, and python-patches. These nine mailing lists were chosen as they are the lead-
ing forums for Python developer discussions, with python-dev being acknowledged as the main forum to discuss PEP
development. The resulting dataset contained a total of 1,542,140 email messages (see Table 1).
   During this step, we also assigned a PEP number to email messages where possible. This was achieved by running
a procedure that identified mentions of PEP numbers (e.g., “PEP 13”) or titles (e.g., “Python Language Governance”)
within each message that could be mapped back to a PEP document. This was stored as a field in the database alongside
other details of a message (author, date, etc.) so that we could easily extract messages that were related specifically to PEP
decision-making.
   One challenge with this is that a single message may mention multiple PEPs, and these mentions could contain impor-
tant information about state changes. For instance, emails in the python-checkins mailing list seldom contain message
where multiple PEPs are checked in together. To resolve this, we split messages whenever a new PEP is mentioned at the
beginning of each paragraph. (Handily, the delimiter “[TAB]” is often used by Python community members in long email
messages to separate discussion of different PEPs.) Thus, a message can be associated with many PEPs.

   We also encountered several messages where neither a PEP number or title was mentioned, but which replied to a mes-

Step 4: Filtering emails exchanged during DM stage

From all the email messages stored in the database, we selected only those that related to PEP decision-making, that
is, emails that had successfully been assigned a PEP number and replies to these messages. The remaining emails were
deemed not to be related to PEP decision-making.


Step 5: Discovering decision-making concepts

We then explored and analysed Python members’ conventions used in email messages containing PEP DM concepts with
the aim of identifying fine-grained states, referred to here as sub-states, that described transitions between the 13 main
states shown in Figure A2. Insights obtained in this step helped us automate extraction of the DM process in later steps.
This step comprised four sub-steps.
    (a) DM concepts in literature: First, we identified from the literature,4,24 a list of seven DM concepts (e.g.,
consensus, majority) used in OSSD, including the well-known Apache Project DM model.4 These concepts, when
embedded in the process diagram, become our sub-states. See the first row of Table 2 for a complete list of these
concepts.
    (b) Manual analysis of one PEP in-detail: Next, we wanted to quickly grasp how decision-making discussions
unfolded in Python, as this would highlight the entities, DM concepts, and conventions used during the development of a
PEP. We therefore studied discussions for one highly contested PEP in detail. To find a suitable PEP, we used the concepts
identified in the previous sub-step, (and obvious variations, e.g., voting, voted, etc.) as keywords to search the raw text (flat
files) of the entire PEP mailing list archive. We then checked these messages for references to specific PEPs. This enabled

    Special Interest Groups (SIGs): While carrying out sub-step 5(c) we found three PEPs (439, 471, and 3144) that
were discussed and decided upon only on the python-disutils-sig mailing list rather than python-dev. Further inves-
tigation revealed that the Python project has many Special Interest Groups (SIGs)26 that support focused efforts
concerning specific functional areas of Python. For example, Distutils-SIG27 focuses on module distribution utilities.
Around 2014, decision-making for PEPs that fell within a SIG’s area of expertise was devolved to the SIGs rather
than requiring all PEPs to go through python-dev, as PEPs discussed there could often “bog down if they address
areas where python-dev lacks significant expertise”.28 Distutils-SIG is thus a decision-making body in its own right
and the PEPs it discusses do not need to be rubber-stamped by the BDFL. Decisions are instead normally made via
a BDFL Delegate (mostly Nick Coghlan, one of the prominent members in the Python community). Of the 40 PEPs
decided by a BDFL Delegate, 22 (10 Standards Track, six Informational, and six Process) were discussed only within
Distutils-SIG, while two further PEPs were discussed in other SIGs. This shows that SIGs are an important element in the
Python DM process.
    Discussions in a SIG mailing list are similar to those in other mailing lists and thus use similar DM concepts. In order
to differentiate DM within SIGs from that through normal channels (python-dev), we created a parallel set of SIG-specific
DM concepts by adding a “SIG” prefix to the 43 already identified DM concepts. Thus there are two versions of each
sub-state, for example, reviewed for the normal DM process and SIG reviewed for the SIG DM process. We found that six
of these SIG-specific DM concepts were used in SIG discussions, as shown in the fourth row of Table 2.
    (d) Semi-automated extraction of remaining DM concepts: As the dataset was significant, we could not manually
study the entire dataset. To make sure we captured all the remaining PEP DM concepts, we utilised Natural Language
Processing (NLP) techniques in the remaining PEP messages.
    (i) Email subject analysis: The email subject line seldom contains the main idea of the message, and therefore pro-
vides an avenue for capturing DM concepts that may not have been stated in the message body. Triple extraction
(discussed shortly) may not capture the concepts from these texts, as these are often not stated as complete sentences,
for example, “PEP 308 vote”. Thus, we used the Stanford Parser to extract the part of speech (POS) tags to find the sub-
ject and verb from these texts. After removing all PEP numbers, PEP titles, numbers, strings containing numerals, and
common message subject prefixes, such as “re”, “fwd”, and “fw”, we used the Stanford Parser to obtain POS tags for
each word in the message subject. Words with a VB, VBG, or VBN POS tags were assigned as verbs, and those with an
NN POS tag as nouns. Once we had all the noun and verb combinations, we manually checked these combinations to
see if we could find any more DM concepts. However, we did not find any additional concepts through analysis of the
message subject.
    (ii) Topic modelling email content for extracting DM concepts: For automatic extraction of DM concepts from Python
mailing list discussions, we utilised topic modelling. This can reduce the need for manually extracting concepts and
extract the remaining concepts (if any). The related terms from topic modelling results could provide us with the DM
concepts related to sub-state extraction. The details and results of our topic modelling approach are attached in Appendix

Step 6: DM concept matching scheme

The DM concepts that we were searching for in sentences of OSSD email messages were commonly stated in the form
of subject (S), verb (V), and object (O), for example, “BDFL” (S), “accepted” (V), “PEP” (O). In our work, these S,V,O
patterns are thus the manifestation of a DM concept that we can capture. We captured all S,V,O patterns corresponding
to the DM concepts identified in step 5 from the 30 manually examined PEPs. These captured patterns were then used to
extract these concepts from the email discussions for the remaining 433 PEPs (see Table 3). Examples of S,V,O triples that
correspond to the concepts of PEP acceptance, consensus, and no majority include “BDFL accepted PEP”, “consensus is
reached”, and “proposal lacks majority support”, respectively.
    For S,V,O (and hence concept) extraction, we tested three Information Extraction (IE) libraries, ClausIE,29 Reverb,30
and Ollie.31 We found that only ClausIE extracted the relevant triples (good triples) that we expected; Reverb and
Ollie in contrast, did not. These findings were consistent with previous studies that compared triple extraction using
these three libraries.32,33 We explain the notion of “relevant triples” as follows. In a study by Rodriguez et al.33 Reverb
and Ollie showed higher precision than ClausIE. However, it was necessary for the IE tool to extract the necessary
triples—this relates to the Recall aspect. In the above-mentioned study, ClausIE extracts more triples than Reverb and
Ollie (in terms of percentage) and is higher in terms of recall. The work of Bast et al.32 also showed that recall for
ClausIE was better than Reverb and Ollie. The results in the study were the same: ClausIE extracted more triples and
necessary triples.
    ClausIE breaks down each sentence into its basic constituents (clauses), and derives triples from these clauses.29
It uses the Stanford Dependency Parser34 to discover the syntactical structure of an input sentence. Table 3 shows
samples of actual concepts (column 2) and corresponding triples (columns 3–5) extracted from PEPs using ClausIE.29
As noted earlier, each concept corresponds to a sub-state. For example, to capture that a BDFL delegate for a
PEP (also known as Benevolent Dictator For One PEP, or BDFOP) has accepted a PEP, the resultant S,V,O triple
has “I” as the subject, “accept” as the verb, and an object comprising all the terms “PEP”, “as”, and “BDFOP”, in
any order.

    We also found that DM concepts were also occasionally stated in the message subjects. These message subjects how-

    Finally, we automatically generated obvious variants of triples already captured, for example, substituting “reject” for
“accept”, or “poll” for “vote”, to ensure that all such variants were captured and all triples were mapped to appropriate
concepts. This process produced a total of 371 unique S,V,O triples representing the 48 DM concepts found by manual
analysis in step 5 (Table 2).


Step 7: Matching DM concepts in PEP discussions

At this stage we were ready to carry out the main DM process extraction. We wanted to determine how and when DM
concepts were expressed in email discussions for all PEPs. The keyword list of concepts from step 5 (Table 2) and the S,V,O
terms from step 6 (Table 3) were provided as inputs to a module that extracted sentences containing these terms from the
89,914 PEP-related messages for all PEPs in our dataset. We also extracted the timestamp of the containing message for
each extracted sentence, which enabled us to identify when a certain DM concept was expressed (e.g., when consensus
was achieved). We call these timestamped sentences matching S,V,O patterns candidate sentences. These details would be
used to create the final process diagram.
    To establish whether each candidate sentence indeed expressed a particular DM concept, we again used ClausIE to
extract S,V,O triples from these sentences. These new triples were then matched with the 371 unique S,V,O triples curated
in the previous step. If any of the triples matched, it established that the sentences exhibited the same DM concept. During
this process we omitted candidate sentences where DM concepts were negated by checking for negation terms anywhere
within the verbs of the captured triple.


Step 8: Post-processing

We removed repeated DM sub-state captures for the same PEP, as these added duplicate sub-states for the same PEP in
the process model. This left us with a total of 9489 confirmed sentences that conveyed the 48 DM concepts identified in
step 5.


Step 9: Visualization

We visualized the results obtained from the earlier steps in the form of process diagrams using the DISCO toolkit35 (e.g.,
see Figure 2).


Step 10: Comparison

We compared and contrasted the extracted overall DM process with the already known processes (Figures A1–A3) using
structural comparative analysis techniques, thus answering RQ1. The results are described in Section 4.1. We then carried
out the same analysis for each of the three PEP types, thus answering RQ2. The results are described in Sections 4.2,4.3,
and 4.4.


Step 11: Evaluation

The evaluation was carried out on two levels. First, we evaluated the framework’s ability to capture DM concepts from sen-
tences. Second, we asked a prominent Python core developer to evaluate the overall DM process model that we extracted

4.1             Extracted overall process model (RQ1)

In this section, we examine the extracted overall PEP DM process model to see what insights it can provide us compared
to previously advertised processes, thus answering RQ1. The overall PEP DM process model is shown in Figure 2. Each
node in the process diagram represents an activity, which can be either a main state or a sub-state. The edges between
nodes represent DM paths among states and sub-states. Note that Figure 2 has been summarized for the sake of clarity.
The full-fledged process model is more complex and can be viewed on Figshare.||
    The goal of RQ1 was to see if the DM process that was implemented differed from the already known processes
(Figures A1–A3). We can immediately see that even the summarized version of the extracted process (Figure 2) is more
detailed than those presented in Figures A1–A3 (also see Tables 6 and 7). Previous studies in particular focused only on
the main DM states of a PEP document.7,8 Our approach here has captured additional main states, sub-states (i.e., states
between the main states), and DM stages, all of which occur in multiple PEPs. To describe these additional DM sub-states
we undertook qualitative analysis of messages belonging to PEPs containing these DM sub-states.


4.1.1             Main states

Previous research reported 13 main states in the Python DM process.7 Our research confirms these states, and a new
Postponed main state was also discovered. However, because this state only occurred once, we feel it was erroneously
used for the Deferred main state. This demonstrates that our method is effective in retrieving all main states have been
previously reported.8


4.1.2             Additional sub-states and DM stages

The 48 sub-states found by our framework are shown in Table 2. Prior research did not include any of these sub-states,7,8
while our manual analysis of the PEP 1 process description (Figure A3) revealed only 17 of them. Thus, our approach has
identified 32 new sub-states (Figure 2). As with the manually-derived process diagram in Figure A3, the newly extracted
sub-states can be grouped into various high-level stages within the overall DM process (e.g., Idea, Consensus Formation).
The process shown in Figure A3 included five stages (dashed red outlines) that were inferred by the authors (i.e., not
explicitly identified in PEP 1). In comparison, our new framework identified nine stages, indicated by dashed blue and
green outlines in Figure 2. The blue stages correspond to the same stages in Figure A3. The green stages are either new, or
replace those identified previously with more focused versions (e.g., Consensus Formation, Voting, and Polling replace the
original Community Feedback stage). The original Review stage has been absorbed into the BDFL/BDFL Delegate Decisions
and SIG BDFL Delegate Decisions stages.

stage, and the second column lists instances of PEPs where these sub-states were identified. We will now discuss these
six DM stages in more detail.

4.1.3          Consensus formation

The Python community’s DM process mostly revolves around forming a community consensus (upper left in Figure 2).
This involves discussion and informal voting. Discussion occurs during both the early development of an idea before it
is formally drafted as a PEP (idea-related sub-states in Table 4), and after a PEP is formally drafted and is reviewed
by the community (discussion-oriented sub-states in Table 4). Our results show that the outcomes of discussions
(outcome-related sub-states in Table 4) can be classified into consensus and no consensus sub-states.
    We also found instances where community discussion entered a cycle, in which certain decision-making sub-states
are revisited multiple times. For example, between the Draft and Accepted main states, a proposal may cycle several times
between the discussion (and informal voting which occurs simultaneously) and ReDraft sub-states (the most extreme
example involving seven such rounds).36 Similarly, a PEP may cycle through the discussion, no consensus, and ReDraft
sub-states, as exemplified by this message by the author for PEP 386 (emphasis added): “As I expected, we did not find a
full consensus on the final PEP 386 schema on Distutils-SIG … So I am proposing to see this PEP discussed for one more
round here, then rejected or approved by the highest authority:”.37 We found many other cycles in the extracted process
model, but these two were the most common ones that involved the newfound sub-states.


4.1.4          Polling

In the Python community, Polling have been taken for a variety of reasons and during various phases of a PEP. These polls
are undertaken when a PEP is still being finalised during Consensus Formation stage, for the entire PEP or for a specific
issue within the PEP that has not received unanimous support. Generally, polls are commonly undertaken to get an
indication of community preferences on a PEP when it is still being completed, and the results of the poll have no impact
on the final decision, for example, PEP 428.38 Another reason is to gain a sense of public opinion prior to a formal vote,
for example, PEP 308.39 For example, during the discussions for PEP 308, the author of the PEP used a poll39 to highlight

between the Informal Voting that occurs during Consensus Formation and Formal Voting as discussed next (i.e., polling is
sometimes treated as formal and sometimes treated as informal).


4.1.5            Voting

Based on our understanding of the Python community, a more formal voting approach is required when it is difficult
to decide on the PEP, based only on preferences expressed via Informal Voting during Consensus Formation, especially
when the Informal Voting may have spanned multiple different versions of a PEP. If necessary, the Python community
uses two voting mechanisms that are more structured in nature. These are Formal (or official) Voting (formal in Table 4),
and Unofficial Complementary Voting (complementary in Table 4).
    Formal voting is used in two scenarios. The first is for deciding on straightforward proposals. On certain occa-
sions, even after the Informal Voting phase, the collective community view may not be certain. PEP 391’s author, for
example, requested a formal vote after informal voting indicated favourable support for the PEP, but it was unclear
whether there was a consensus.46 Similarly, PEP279 had previously gotten positive feedback, but an issue remained, so
the author held a formal vote before submitting the PEP for BDFL approval. Another purpose of formal voting in the
Python community is to facilitate a collective decision on proposals which have several syntaxes as options and when
consensus cannot be reached on these during the Informal Voting phase. Formal voting, like polling, seeks a commu-
nity majority. In the official vote for PEP308, for example, there were eight alternative syntaxes to select from, but the
results indicated no majority, and the PEP was later rejected by the BDFL as there was no “overwhelming majority
for change”.47
    Unofficial Complementary Voting (UCV) is used to complement the results of a formal vote by conducting a dif-
ferent type of ballot, and using a different method (Condorcet’s Method) to tally the results.48 Another difference
is that while it is clear that the complementary vote is also not a binding vote, when combined with the formal
vote “both sets of results … should be more useful to the BDFL than either one would be alone”.49 For example,
PEP 308 included both formal and complementary votes, but neither found a majority, and the PEP was rejected by
the BDFL.
    Apart from the voting mechanisms, there was also discussion on voting schemes (discussion in Table 4). With
PEP 308, there were discussions in the community about choosing between IRV and the Condorcet methods.
The community discussed various voting schemes, including ballot and tally methods (e.g., Condorcet’s Method
vs. Approval Voting).50
    In terms of communication platforms for voting, both official and unofficial were initiated in the main discussion
mailing list, followed by emailing votes (PEP 308) or through a special website and in a newsgroup (comp.lang.python)—as
in case of PEP 318. The voting balloting and tally procedure in the Python community is thorough. Most voting have
simple ballot procedures, similar to polls, however, in PEP 308 there was a ranked ballot, where members had to specify

4.1.6       BDFL and BDFL delegate decisions

Our qualitative analysis of the 30 PEPs covered all PEPs that used Polling, Formal Voting, and only some of the con-
troversial PEPs that used Informal voting. From this set of PEPs, we have observed that when making decisions, the
collective community view (implies the views of the core-developers), based either on the results from consensus forma-
tion (i.e., through Informal Voting) or the majority view (i.e., through Polling and Formal Voting) are indeed taken into
consideration by the BDFL. The majority of the proposals that used Polling and all forms of voting are decided, based on
the collective community view. However, as we have outlined before, the preferences of Python community members in
these DM schemes are non-binding. The BDFL does reserve the right to make a PEP decision by himself or to overturn a
consensus or majority outcome.
     PEP 326 is one example of the BDFL making a decision that overrode the community majority formed after the
Informal Voting phase. The results were 12 for and four against the proposal.52 A majority wanted the feature to be imple-
mented, but the BDFL rejected the PEP with detailed reasons: “This has lasted long enough. Python is not a democracy.
So here’s a BDFL Pronouncement. PEP 326 is REJECTED, because … ”.53 The BDFL goes on to list the technical issues
he has with the PEP.
     In regard to Polling, most outcomes were used indirectly by the BDFL while making PEP decisions. PEP 255 was
accepted by BDFL, regardless of no majority. PEP 3103 was rejected as there was no majority. The lack of a majority in
PEP 318 forced it into another round of discussions. The polls in PEP 428 and 488 were for a syntax in the PEP, and the
preferred syntax in polls for both PEPs were chosen as part of the respective PEPs.
     In relation to Formal Voting, most decisions were made based on their outcomes. The BDFL accepted PEP 279 as more
votes were in favour. After many positive votes, the BDFL accepted the PEP 392. Similarly, PEP 308 had a long discussion,
where all the DM schemes were used: Informal Voting, Polling, and Formal Voting (both an Official, and a Complementary
Vote). All these schemes could not come up with a community consensus or a clear majority. Thus, the PEP 308 was
initially rejected as “no clear majority” after the Formal Voting. However, after two years, the idea in the PEP resurfaced,
and the BDFL eventually accepted the PEP without requiring community preference, again while emphasising the fact
that he was still the BDFL: “It’s still my language!:-)”.54
     We can draw two conclusions in relation to the BDFL’s consideration of the collective community view (via consensus
or majority). First, while the votes in Polling and all forms of voting are non-binding, it is evident from the outcomes above
that in the Python community, the PEP decisions made by the BDFL are normally made indirectly, based on the outcomes
of Informal Voting, Polling or Formal Voting. However, the Formal Voting process has a more formal thrust than the Polling
process. Second, it is evident that while there are some cases where the BDFL overrides the collective community view,
in the majority of the proposals we analysed in this section, his decisions reflect the collective view of the community
(i.e., of the core-developers).
     The next section highlights certain occasions where BDFL Delegates are allowed to make PEP decisions. Based on the
findings in this section and the next, the BDFL and the BDFL Delegates make most of decisions on the final outcome of a
proposal (accept or reject). Generally the Python community continues into new rounds of redrafts and discussions when
consensus cannot be reached on a PEP. However, if the community is divided on a proposal and cannot be reconciled,
it becomes necessary for the BDFL to intervene and make the PEP decision. For instance, after a disagreement in the
community the BDFL accepted PEP 285.37

4.1.7       SIG consensus formation and SIG BDFL delegate decisions

The activities for SIG Consensus Formation were similar to those for PEPs that go through the regular channels (i.e.,
python-dev rather than a Special Interest Group mailing list). We found the following six DM sub-states in the SIG mailing
lists: SIG requests feedback, SIG reviewed, SIG consensus, SIG ready BDFL review, SIG BDFL Delegate reviewed, SIG BDFL
Delegate Accepted PEP. The presence of the SIG consensus sub-state indicates that even in SIGs a collective view is still
preferred. Furthermore, the presence of the SIG BDFL Delegate sub-states and absence of any of the SIG BDFL sub-states
(see Table 4) signifies that decisions are made only by the BDFL Delegate assigned to those PEPs. This implies that the
BDFL has less influence on PEPs that go through SIGs, and may even imply that the BDFL cannot reject PEPs introduced
in SIGs.
     We will compare our extracted process model in detail against the three previously published models (Figures A1–A3)
in Section 4.4, but even a cursory visual comparison of Figures A1–A3 with Figure 2 shows that our extracted process
model includes many more activities (i.e., main states and sub-states), paths, and stages than the prior models. Thus, our
extracted process model exposes fine-grained information about DM sub-states and stages that were hidden within the
data.

4.2       DM process models for each PEP type (RQ2a)

In addition to unearthing the overall enacted DM process for Python, our DeMaP Miner framework was also used to
discover the DM processes for each of the three PEP types: Process, Informational and Standards Track. The corresponding
process models are shown in Figures 3,4, and 5, respectively. In this section, we compare the similarities and differences
of these three process models, thus answering RQ2a.
    Table 6 summarizes the stages that appear in the DM process model for each of the three PEP types. We can see that
Consensus Formation, BDFL/BDFL Delegate Decisions, SIG Consensus Formation, and SIG BDFL Delegate Decisions are in
common across all PEP types. This could be reflective of a DM model that is a combination of the meritocracy-based and
the dictatorship-based DM principles.
    At the same time, there are some differences between the respective DM process models. The DM models for Informa-
tional PEPs and Process PEPs are similar, while the DM process model for Standards Track PEPs is much more complex
compared to the other two PEP types. The DM process model for Standards Track PEPs adds Polling and Voting stages
(including official and complementary voting, and voting method discussion). These are all geared towards achieving a
community majority.
    Our findings therefore reveals that the underlying DM processes for the three PEP types are different. This is likely due
to the purposes behind each of the PEP types. As Standards Track PEPs deal with the core aspect of the Python community,
that is, the evolution of the language, it is more complex to make decisions on them. Consequently they deal with highly
technical topics that are prone to many differing opinions. They are also the most common PEP type, comprising about
75% of all PEPs, and are therefore the largest target for decision-making in Python.
    In contrast, Informational PEPs are much less common (about 16% of all PEPs) and deal with dissemination of infor-
mation, so they are less likely to require complex decision-making processes. Process PEPs are even less common (about
9% of all PEPs) and define the Python community’s processes. Both types are less likely to attract many differing opinions,
which means that the community is unlikely to be presented with multiple conflicting options during the development of
Informational or Process PEPs. This lessens the likelihood of an impasse or lack of consensus, thus effectively eliminating
the need for DM stages like Voting or Polling.

5             EVALUATION

This section presents evidence both for the robustness of the findings that were used to construct the process diagram in
Figure 2, and for the utility of this extracted process diagram. In Section 5.1 we discuss how we evaluated our framework’s
accuracy at correctly identifying the DM concepts embedded in a sentence. In Section 5.2, we discuss how we solicited
stakeholder feedback on the overall extracted decision-making process model.


5.1               Framework effectiveness

For evaluating our frameworks accuracy in mining DM concepts, three raters (the authors of this paper) judged whether or
not sentences labelled by the framework accurately contained the labelled concept (e.g., consensus reached). A total of 200
random sentences were selected from the corpus of all sentences for this evaluation. Each rater also counted the number
of true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN) produced by the framework
for these sentences. The first author (moderator) described how to rate the sentences to the other two raters before the
evaluation began, followed by a training session in which examples of selected concepts with matching related sentences
were discussed and shown.
    As included in Table 8A, Cohen’s kappa coefficient (𝜅) was used to assess inter-rater reliability (IRR) between the
three raters (R1 , R2 and R3 ). This metric assesses the observed level of agreement amongst raters of a certain set of nominal
values and accounts for any agreements that may have occurred by chance. Table 8B shows how to interpret the kappa
value.

    We can see that kappa is at least 0.67 across all three raters, indicating substantial agreement. We also computed
the Fleiss coefficient55 which is widely used for measuring IRR between more than two raters. The Fleiss coefficient
was 0.73, indicating good agreement56 and is close to the excellent agreement threshold of 0.75. We can therefore be
confident that the raters were generally consistent in correctly labelling DM concepts in the 200 sample sentences. We then
resolved inconsistencies between the raters by group discussion and consensus. After reconciling these inconsistencies,
the agreement level was 1.0 (perfect).
    Table 9 shows the results of the framework’s evaluation. The TP, TN, FP, and FN scores are shown on the left side
of Table 9, while the right side shows the accuracy, precision, recall, and F-score of our framework’s recognition of DM
states compared to a human (i.e., as agreed by the three raters). The evaluation shows that our framework’s performance
compares favourably to a human with an accuracy of 82%.
    There were 35 instances where labels were captured incorrectly, producing false positives and false negatives. Some
of these were due to incomplete sentences and incorrect grammar in the text of messages. The members of OSSD com-
munities discuss similar issues every day, therefore it is easy for them to quickly understand each other’s messages even
though their messages may not be grammatically correct. Such instances are challenging for an NLP toolkit, however.
Other incorrect labels were a result of limitations in ClausIE’s triple extraction, which does not always correctly extract
all necessary S,V,O triples.
    A third source of false positives and negatives is that we were unable to capture all potential negation phrases, such as
nuanced negation terms expressing sophisticated human cognition. For example, the following sentence is a false positive
for consensus, as it actually exhibits both consensus and no consensus (arguing): ’My impression of the discussion is that
the consensus so far is ‘maybe’ and ’it’s too painful’, and that’s why we’re still arguing.’57

7       DISCUSSION

7.1       A framework for mining DM processes

The overarching research objective of this paper was to design and develop an approach that extracts the
decision-making process based on the data available in email discussions In meeting this objective, our study
provides a computational framework that can extract the DM processes that OSS project technical propos-
als traverse during their life cycle. The DeMaP Miner framework implements several steps built around information
retrieval and NLP techniques (see Figure 1), which aim to automate DM process extraction. Using the framework, we
have extracted the detailed Python DM processes from the email repositories, and identified the many sub-states and
stages that occur within this process which were previously hidden in email messages.
    We have evaluated the results of our framework in two respects. The system evaluation has shown that the framework
performs comparably to a human with an accuracy of 82%. Some of the sources of inaccuracy were due to incomplete
sentences and incorrect grammar, some were limitations in capturing all possible negation terms from complex sentences,
while others were a result of limitations in ClausIE’s triple extraction. While we may not have been able to capture all
instances of each DM sub-state, we believe that in general, the DM process flow (i.e., the flow between states and sub-states
and among sub-states) that we have captured for each PEP type and for all PEPs overall is representative of how this
actually occurs.
    The stakeholder evaluation of the simplified version of the Python DM process model (Figure 2) was carried out by a
Python core developer. This has shown that the extracted process model was indeed useful and provided more information
on understanding the PEP DM process, compared to the published process (Figure A3). These evaluations have two impli-
cations. First, they validate our extracted PEP decision-making process model. Second, they imply that our DeMaP Miner
framework is adequate in extracting DM processes from Python repositories. Thus, it has the potential to be extended into

    The reason that the extracted DM process is more sophisticated than the original PEP 1 process17 could be due to
the PEP DM model’s evolution through time. A static model is difficult to sustain in an evolving software development
project.62,63 In the early days of Python when community was small, the founder made all of the choices, but as it devel-
oped, community members’ opinions became more significant. (although the majority of final decisions remained in the
hands of the project founder, as indicated by our findings). Thus, the process guidelines needed to be flexible in order to
accommodate further changes in the project, leading to greater process complexity.
    For example, in 2013 the Python community realized that some PEPs could be managed better if discussions were
shifted to separate specialized mailing lists, and decisions on them delegated to the experts in the relevant subject areas.
This reduced the cognitive workload of the project leader and community members, who may not have the specialized
knowledge required to properly engage with these PEPs,28 and allowed for faster decisions to be made. In terms of group
decision-making, the fact that PEPs from the Distutils-SIG were managed only by members of that group highlights that
even under a dictatorship model, Python allowed some expert members to make decisions on PEPs, and does point to a
somewhat more collective decision-making approach. More recently, the members of the Python Steering Council con-
firmed that an objective of the council-based model is to defer decisions on PEPs to “a chosen expert among the core
developers (or sometimes outside of that group)”, thus making delegation the default rather than the exception.64 This
will enable a wider range of people to become involved in shaping the future of Python.
    As emphasized in prior research,7 the publication of an accurate and thorough decision-making process model, such
as ours (Figure 2), can lead to a more “informed” community proposal workflow, where everyone’s expectations regarding
DM procedures are clear to everyone. When project managers or leaders and junior members have access to such mod-
els, they can avoid becoming disheartened (due to a lack of understanding of the different processes involved) or perhaps
abandoning the project entirely.7 Overall, our model depicts decision-making holistically, allowing for a better compre-
hension of the overall DM process, which was previously restricted to document-centric state diagrams (Figures A1 and
A2) and the manually created process model (Figure A3).

7.4       Implications

Using our DeMaP Miner framework, we have extracted, identified, and offered a descriptive insight into the ‘actual’ DM
processes of OSS enhancement proposals. The extracted information would be helpful in assisting stakeholders and new
community members to understand the underlying governance model. We found additional DM processes (presented as
sub-states in this paper), beyond those documented and publicised in the Python project—that considers both: the view
of the community and that of the dictator. The key processes we have unearthed include Consensus Formation, Polling
and Formal Voting. We also found that consensus formation and the project leader’s decisions are common DM phases
for all proposals, which is a new finding in the literature.
    This study also includes findings on some additional aspects related to DM, based on qualitative analysis, which was
missing in the literature on OSSD DM. For the aforementioned three key processes, it reports on how votes are cast (ballot)
and counted (tally), and the various communication platforms used. Particularly, it highlights the role of the benevolent
dictator in an OSS project with a mixed meritocracy and a dictatorship DM approach. We found that most of the decisions
made by the dictator were based on the collective community view. This collective view is mostly the preference of the
core developers, gained either through a consensus or a majority. We have also highlighted a few instances when the
project leader overrode the collective view.
    We believe that these findings also have implications for practice and theory, which are considered here. Given that
transparency in DM has been identified as a critical factor for the success of OSSD projects,3 the incompleteness in
previous DM process models noted in our results could be unfavourable to the successful decision-making in Python,
and in other OSSD projects where this phenomenon exists. Therefore, based on our findings, we believe that publish-
ing our extracted Python DM process will make the actual DM processes transparent and help stakeholders, junior and
future members of the community to in quickly grasping how decisions are made in the Python community, especially
regarding the more subtle nuances of the process. For example, while the acceptance of PEPs is based on commu-
nity consensus, there are some instances where the BDFL has approved a PEP despite a lack of consensus. This aspect
of the Python DM process is not revealed by examining the previously disclosed DM models (Figure A1), which is
state-based (i.e., it contains only main states). The BDFL’s capacity to overrule community consensus stemmed from
the Python community’s monarchist model, although despite this, instances of the BDFL overriding community consen-
sus were uncommon (which is in line with the BDFL’s “benevolent” aspect). Again, this is not clear from the originally
published process.

7.5       Limitations, threats to validity and future work

This section describes the limitations and threats to validity of our work, as posed by our research method, and the actions
that we took to mitigate them.


7.5.1       Limitations

This study developed organically out of a need to answer specific research questions based on the available data set about
PEP development. As such, it has several limitations which we aim to address in future work.
    First, while carrying out our exploratory analysis of states, sub-states, and DM stages in the Python DM process, we
manually analysed email messages relating to only 30 PEPs rather than all PEPs (which would have been prohibitively
time-consuming). However, we reached data saturation (no new concepts were found) after analysing messages for 20
PEPS. We therefore believe that we have captured all the important concepts in Python DM. The positive stakeholder
evaluation from the Python steering council member supports this. However, we concede that we may have missed some
Python DM concepts.
    Second, some DM concepts may not have been captured as our framework relies on the triple extraction capa-
bilities of information extraction libraries. Although these libraries do not require any background knowledge, they
do not always extract all the subjects, verbs, and objects from sentences. We believe this is a limitation for any NLP
research, however, not just ours. For this reason, we place more emphasis in our work on comparisons of DM stages
rather than individual sub-states. Third, while Python email archives are the official communication repository of the
Python OSSD community, we have noted that developers sometimes opt for a faster means of interaction, such as a
chat-based system (e.g., the freenode IRC network), social networking sites (e.g., Twitter), or face-to-face interactions.
Unfortunately, transcripts of these communications are not available for us to extract more useful information about
decision-making.
    Fourth, a limitation of the process mining techniques so far developed is the huge number of potential activities (i.e.,
states and sub-states) and paths between these activities. In the fully fledged version of our overall DM process diagram for
all PEPs, there are 61 activities and 235 different paths between these activities. Figure 2 presents a summarized version
of this process model, showing only 20% of paths for readability. This enabled us to visualize a structured process model
for all PEPs. However, we are mindful that the application of our framework to a different OSSD repository may produce
a larger set of activities and paths, leading to a highly complex process diagram. Process mining is a visual technology,
most usefully applied in close collaboration between (in our case) OSSD decision-makers and process mining experts.
To resolve and comprehend this large amount of information, we need to involve domain experts at regular intervals in
summarizing these process models in order to develop the most useful representations and visualizations of DM in these
communities.


7.5.2       Threats to internal validity

As discussed in Section 5.1, we evaluated the inter-rater reliability of three human raters at identifying sentences that
contained DM concepts. The Cohen’s kappa coefficient (before reconciliation) was at least 0.67 across all three raters,
indicating substantial agreement. Similarly, the Fleiss coefficient was 0.73, indicating “good” agreement.56 After recon-
ciling the inconsistencies, the agreement was 100%. This was then used as the baseline for calculating the accuracy of our
framework at classifying DM concepts (82%). We are therefore confident that this is a true reflection of our framework’s
capability.

7.5.3       Threats to external validity

The main threat to external validity is the generalization of our DeMaP Miner framework to other OSS projects.
In this work, we are limited to the investigation of a single case study involving Python, due to the sheer volume
of information that needs to be manually analysed. In the future, we intend to study other OSS projects in order
to get a broader understanding of their decision-making processes and reasons behind decisions. We are particu-
larly interested in the OpenJDK project, whose DM structure is inspired by Python, but also in other communities
that have a BDFL, like Linux and Perl. For this purpose we have made the DeMaP Miner framework as generic
as possible, with parameter files enabling variable values such as the proposal identifier (e.g., “PEP”, “JEP”) to be
configured.


7.5.4       Future research directions

This section outlines avenues for future research in relation to finding out more on people and processes during DM in
OSSD.

• Extracting DM sub-states from other OSSD repositories: In the future, we also plan to extend the use of our
  DeMaP Miner framework to extract fine-grained DM processes, which lie hidden in large email repositories of other
  OSS projects, including those that employ community-based DM models such as PostgreSQL, and those with monar-
  chist DM models such as Linux and Perl. These investigations will help us understand how decisions are made in
  these projects and to gain a broader understanding of the DM process in OSSD in general. We also intend to study how
  DM processes change as projects mature. As discussed in literature,1 dictatorship-based OSS projects in time tend to
  embrace more open and democratic systems (e.g., Linux).
      For these purposes, we intend to generalise the DeMaP Miner framework so that it works in other OSSD reposito-
  ries, starting with the OpenJDK community. The OpenJDK DM process is based on that of Python, but with additional
  states.65 Regardless, first, a detailed manual study of emails belonging to a portion of the OpenJDK proposals to grasp
  how the DM processes are expressed will be required.
• Structuring unstructured OSSD repositories for analysis: When applying the DeMaP miner framework to other
  OSSD repositories, we would certainly encounter OSS projects which do not have proposal numbers. Examples are
  Apache, Perl and Linux, where proposals are referred to by their title, not a number. Thus, it would be more difficult
  to trace discussions relating to a proposal. A future study could be carried out using computational techniques such as
  clustering to identify similar proposal titles. Topic matching approaches can also provide an approximate judgement of
  these.

in general. We evaluated the accuracy of the DeMaP Miner framework at identifying Python DM concepts and found that
it was comparable to that of human raters, with an overall accuracy of 82%. We also undertook a stakeholder evaluation
with a Python Steering Council member, who confirmed that the enacted process that we extracted was similar to the
actual DM process used. Based on the positive results of these two evaluations, we believe that our framework is effective
at unearthing hidden DM processes. With appropriate modifications, the framework can be used to extract PEP-like DM
processes in other communities, such as OpenJDK’s Java Enhancement Proposals (JEPs). This will be of great benefit to
researchers interested in studying OSSD decision-making processes.

APPENDIX A. DECISION MAKING IN PYTHON OSSD COMMUNITY

The Python community has publicised the set of states a PEP document goes through in PEP 1.17 Figure A1 describes PEP
1 and the main states within the process used to decide on the outcome of PEPs. The figure shows the high-level process;
there are eight possible states that a proposal can go through before being made Final, Rejected or Withdrawn. Accepted
PEPs that propose changes to Python itself are marked as ‘final’ once implementation of the changes is completed, thus
freezing the PEP and prohibiting further changes. The only way to change a finalised PEP is to create a new PEP that
supersedes it. Accepted Process PEPs describe processes and conventions (such as voting): however, they are often “living
documents” that can be changed as needed. These are marked as Active instead of Final and remain active forever.

    We implemented TM as part of our exploratory study as our intuition was that there would be at least some
cases where events describing a particular topic-specific scenario (DM concept in our case) tend to be dependent on
each other and may exist in the same sentence. Therefore, TM can be used to identify these terms (e.g., a sentence
about the BDFL making a pronouncement on a PEP can refer to the BDFL Pronouncement, reject, no support, etc.).
Using these terms we can also extract more sentences from the repository to further understand DM on PEPs. This
automated approach is to capture both PEP sub-states that we do not know (for example, BDFL Pronouncement in
PEPs). We tested TM on the 30 manually examined PEPs (outlined in Section 3) first, before moving onto the entire
PEP repository.
    There are some challenges to applying TM in software repositories, which related to extracting information from the
email message body. The main challenge is that there are too many programming terms within the email messages. These
terms show up in TM results. Sample coding terms include, “while”, “for”, “function”, “disk”, and “network”. At first, we
attempted to add these coding terms to the stopword list and conducted the TM again. After a large number of attempts
and re-runs, it was obvious that this was a time-consuming task. A better solution had to be created, which we describe
below.
    Essentially, we aim to identify a list of contingency relations between two events: the various DM states (and sub-states)
and the cause behind the transition between states. A contingency relationship is a relationship between two events, one
being “contingent” or a consequence of the other event. An example contingency relationship in the Python DM is the
Accepted state which is a direct result of the consensus sub-state. There are some studies reported in the literature which
aim to identify contingency relations from text automatically. In this part of our study, we borrow some steps from the
methodology presented by Riaz et al.67 Our initial steps are quite elaborate as we intend to extract contingency relations
from OSSD email messages.
    Step a–Identifying sentences containing the word “PEP”: For all email messages belonging to each PEP, we
extracted all sentences which have the term “PEP”. We believe that the sub-states would be stated in these sentences, or
the sentences before and after these sentences. We therefore extracted the previous, current and next paragraphs of this
sentence and stored these into a database table. Our intuition is through this approach some candidate sentences and
terms were bound to be found.
    Step b–Removing coding terms: Since there were numerous coding terms in the email messages, instead of iter-
atively removing these terms, we considered only words in the English dictionary and left out the rest. We used a good
source68 for the set of all English words. This list is more comprehensive we found and the list also contained each term’s
lemmatised version. The ignored terms were stored in another table for later checking if they included any essential terms
related to DM concepts.
    Step c–Configuring TM parameters: Afterwards, we ran the TM on these extracted terms. We began TM with a
configuration of 20 topics and 20 terms for each topic, and then tested with little variations in these values. The main
criteria was that the co-occurring terms within the same topic in the TM results should be terms used by members to
convey the same DM concept, or main state. For instance, terms such as “pronouncement”, “consensus”, “support” in
the same topic denotes the Accepted main state. The objective of this TM approach is that, if rows in TM results include
several terms associated with the same DM concept, a DM concept as a label can be assigned for each row. We achieved
the best TM results with 25 topics and 15 terms. On these final results, we labelled assigned a DM concept as a label for
each topic.
