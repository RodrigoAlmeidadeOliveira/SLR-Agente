\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Software development teams live with a question that is simple to ask and
hard to answer honestly: \emph{when will this be done?}
This thesis attacks that question with honesty about uncertainty:
it does not promise a single calendar date; it delivers a distribution of
plausible dates with probabilities (for example P50, P85, P95), grounded
in how work actually moves through the process rather than in averages of
completed items alone~\cite{vacanti2015}.

Figure~\ref{fig:cap1-problem-context} anchors the problem before any
formalism.
On the environment side, work is not instantaneous: each task typically
traverses stages such as
\textit{To Do} $\rightarrow$ \textit{In Progress} $\rightarrow$
\textit{Review} $\rightarrow$ \textit{Done}, waits in queues, returns for
rework, or stalls.
The \emph{path} taken---not only the final count of completions---carries
information about remaining effort and risk.
On the data side, contemporary tools (Git, Jira, CI/CD, code review)
already record each movement automatically: when a case entered, left, or
returned a stage.
Those records form an \emph{event log}---a digital trace of the real
Software Development Lifecycle (SDLC), from task opening to
delivery~\cite{cook1998,rubin2007,poncin2011process}---not a subjective
estimate.

\input{capitulos/fig_cap1_problem_context}

The practical question then becomes: how does industry use this data
today, and why is that still insufficient?
In practice, delivery forecasts still rely largely on aggregated
repository metrics (velocity, throughput, cycle time) or on
throughput-based Monte Carlo simulation that samples weekly completion
counts and projects backlog burn-down~\cite{vacanti2015}.
Such methods observe \emph{how many} items finish; they do not model
\emph{how} items move (Figure~\ref{fig:cap1-throughput-blackbox}).
A task in Review is treated like a task in To Do; rework loops and
bottlenecks remain invisible; the forecast yields a number without a
process-level explanation that managers can act upon.
The operational pain that motivates this thesis is therefore concrete:
\textbf{process-unaware delivery forecasting under uncertainty}---
forecasts of software development outcomes that ignore process structure
and therefore remain poorly calibrated, hard to explain, and weakly
actionable.

Process mining, as formulated by van der Aalst and consolidated in the
Process Mining Manifesto~\cite{aalst2012process,aalst2016process},
provides techniques to discover models, check conformance, and enhance
processes from event data, spanning descriptive, diagnostic, predictive,
and prescriptive uses.
In healthcare, manufacturing, finance, and public administration these
techniques have supported operational decisions~\cite{aalst2012process}.
Software engineering exhibits a paradox: it generates among the most
high-resolution time-stamped event data of any organizational domain, yet
applications of process mining to the SDLC largely remain at the
descriptive level (discovery), while forecasting practice remains
structurally blind~\cite{cook1998,poncin2011process,rubin2007}.

This thesis \textbf{proposes} PATHCAST (PATH-aware foreCASTing): open the
black box by learning the real process structure from flow data
(Figure~\ref{fig:cap1-pathcast-aware}).
PATHCAST integrates process mining, Markov-chain estimation, Monte Carlo
simulation, and machine learning into a single analytical pipeline
(Figure~\ref{fig:cap1-three-tools}).
Throughout this thesis, \emph{pipeline} denotes one of three clearly
distinguished senses:
(i)~the \textbf{PATHCAST pipeline}---the four-stage method composition
(Definition~\ref{def:pipeline} in Chapter~\ref{ch:method});
(ii)~a \textbf{CI/CD pipeline}---an automated build/delivery toolchain,
never abbreviated as a standalone ``pipeline'' without the CI/CD qualifier;
(iii)~the SLR label \textbf{L2 (Pipeline)}---Integration Level~2 in
Chapter~\ref{ch:slr}, a maturity category, not a software architecture.
The method transforms heterogeneous development-process data into
structured event logs, discovers process models, estimates state-transition
dynamics (including \emph{measured} rework probabilities), and produces
distributional, process-aware forecasts that respect where each case sits
in the flow and that can point to structural sources of delay.

\section{Research Problem}
\label{sec:problem}

Three interrelated problems motivate this research.
For each problem we state (i)~the operational pain it generates,
(ii)~related work that attempts to reduce that pain, and
(iii)~the residual limitation that PATHCAST targets.
We label them \emph{descriptive ceiling}, \emph{structural blindness},
and \emph{technical fragmentation}.

\subsection{Descriptive ceiling in software process mining}

\paragraph{Pain.}
Teams obtain maps of how work flowed in the past, but not probabilistic
statements about how work will finish under uncertainty.
Without that step, process mining in the SDLC does not support the
delivery question that motivates this thesis.

\paragraph{What exists.}
Early applications of process discovery to software development process
repositories---before
and around the consolidation of the Process Mining
Manifesto~\cite{aalst2012process}---showed that process models can be
extracted from development
data~\cite{cook1998,rubin2007,poncin2011process}.
Those studies establish feasibility of discovery on SDLC logs; they do
not claim predictive or prescriptive process intelligence.

\paragraph{Limitation.}
Published work in this line largely stops at description: it reveals
what happened, but does not advance from discovered process structure to
stochastic modeling and calibrated probabilistic forecasting of SDLC
outcomes.
The ceiling is therefore not a lack of discovery algorithms, but the
absence of a validated path from discovery to distributional prediction
in software development.

\subsection{Structural blindness of throughput-based forecasting}

\paragraph{Pain.}
Throughput is simply how many tasks a team finishes per week.
The dominant market method for agile delivery forecasting samples that
historical series, draws random weeks thousands of times, and projects
when the backlog will burn down~\cite{vacanti2015}.
Figure~\ref{fig:cap1-throughput-blackbox} summarizes the structural
failure: the method sees only a \emph{count} of completions---a black box
that observes \emph{how many} and ignores \emph{how}.
It treats every week as statistically exchangeable, yet real processes
change (rework bursts, review bottlenecks, holidays, scope change);
it cannot distinguish a case almost done in Review from one that has
barely started in To Do; and it returns a number without a cause, so
managers cannot act on the delaying stage.

\input{capitulos/fig_cap1_throughput_blackbox}

\paragraph{What exists.}
Vacanti~\cite{vacanti2015} popularized throughput sampling for backlog
completion forecasts.
Whittaker and Thomason~\cite{whittaker1994} used Markov models in
software testing, but without process-mining-discovered SDLC flows.
Neither line opens the black box of the development workflow with
empirically discovered transition structure.

\paragraph{Limitation.}
Throughput Monte Carlo is, at root, a sophisticated average: it assumes
a generative process that does not hold in practice.
Internal transitions, measured rework probabilities, state-specific
delays, and path-dependent variability remain outside the model.
An item reworked three times is statistically identical to one that
progresses linearly.
Variance explained by process dynamics is therefore unavailable to the
forecast or to the decision maker.
PATHCAST's proposal is precisely to open that black box
(Figure~\ref{fig:cap1-pathcast-aware}): each state is a stage; each arc
carries a probability \emph{estimated} from the log---including measured
rework loops---so the forecast knows where each case is, models the path,
and can point to bottlenecks.

\input{capitulos/fig_cap1_pathcast_aware}

\subsection{Technical fragmentation across complementary techniques}

\paragraph{Pain.}
The ingredients of process-aware probabilistic forecasting already exist
separately, yet practitioners and researchers lack a formalized,
empirically validated composition for the SDLC.
Ad hoc glue code does not provide contracts between stages, shared
evaluation protocols, or reusable guarantees.

\paragraph{What exists.}
Process mining supplies discovery and conformance~\cite{aalst2016process};
Markov chains supply interpretable transition structure;
Monte Carlo supplies full predictive distributions beyond expected
times~\cite{vacanti2015};
machine learning supplies non-linear signals (for example code churn or
review latency) that a purely stochastic stage may miss~\cite{hassan2008road}.
Predictive and prescriptive process monitoring surveys map related
techniques in generic BPM settings~\cite{marquezChamorro2018,kubrak2022},
and automated discovery surveys map algorithms without an SDLC
forecasting pipeline~\cite{augusto2019}.
Figure~\ref{fig:cap1-three-tools} states the methodological rationale:
each technique answers a question the others do not; removing any one
loses a capability.

\input{capitulos/fig_cap1_three_tools}

\paragraph{Limitation.}
Integration of process mining $\rightarrow$ Markov estimation
$\rightarrow$ Monte Carlo $\rightarrow$ machine learning into one
PATHCAST pipeline with explicit inter-stage contracts, validated on
SDLC logs, has not---to the best of our knowledge, and within the corpus
reviewed in Chapter~\ref{ch:slr}---been established as a named, reproducible
method in
software engineering.
The three tools were not chosen as fashion:
Markov answers \emph{what is the structure?};
Monte Carlo answers \emph{and the uncertainty?} (full distributions beyond
expected sojourn times);
machine learning answers \emph{what else helps predict?} and operationalizes
H1 on process-derived versus traditional MSR features.
Hypothesis~H3 (Section~\ref{sec:rqs}) tests whether the combination
outperforms isolated use---the SDLC integration gap left empty in the SLR
($L3=0$ among 340 distinct confirmed studies) that this thesis occupies.

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

\subsection{General Objective}

This thesis \textbf{proposes} a computational method for the probabilistic
forecasting of software development processes, based on the integration of
process mining, stochastic modeling through Markov chains, Monte Carlo
simulation, and machine learning, capable of transforming data from
software development process repositories into analytical models that
enable prediction of the behavior and outcomes of these processes---in
particular, distributional forecasts of execution time, trajectory, and
delivery-related outcomes under uncertainty.

\subsection{Hypotheses}

The following hypotheses guide the evaluation design.
They are stated here before the specific objectives so that the
objectives can refer to them without circularity.

\textbf{H1 (Predictive Superiority of Process-Derived Features):}
\emph{Predictive models that incorporate features derived from process
structure achieve superior predictive performance compared with models
based solely on traditional software repository metrics.}

\textbf{H2 (Fidelity and Interpretability of Markov-Based Stochastic Models):}
\emph{Stochastic models based on Markov chains enable a more faithful
representation of the dynamics of software development processes,
allowing the generation of interpretable probabilistic forecasts.}

\textbf{H3 (Performance Gain from Multi-Technique Integration):}
\emph{The integration of process mining, Monte Carlo simulation, and
machine learning improves forecast quality when compared with the
isolated use of each of these techniques.}

\subsection{Specific Objectives}

To achieve the general objective and to test hypotheses H1--H3, the
following specific objectives are defined:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Build structured event logs from data originating from software
    development process repositories---including issue tracking systems,
    version control repositories, and continuous integration
    (CI/CD) pipelines---in order to formally represent the actual
    behavior of development processes.

    \item Apply process mining techniques to discover models that
    represent the actual flow of activities in software development,
    allowing the identification of states, transitions, and execution
    patterns of the process.

    \item Develop a probabilistic prediction model based on Markov chains
    and Monte Carlo simulation, capable of estimating execution-time
    distributions, process trajectories, and possible outcomes of
    development processes (addressing the structural blindness of
    throughput-only forecasts).

    \item Empirically evaluate the proposed method by integrating it with
    machine learning techniques and comparing it with predictive models
    based solely on traditional software repository metrics, analyzing
    gains in predictive accuracy and interpretability.
    This objective connects the operational pain (process-unaware
    forecasting) and the research gap (absence of a validated L3
    integration on SDLC data; Chapter~\ref{ch:slr}) to a controlled
    comparison that tests H1--H3.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Justification and Research Gap}
\label{sec:justification}

The principal pain remains process-unaware delivery forecasting under
uncertainty (Section~\ref{sec:problem}).
Adjacent surveys and methods each reduce part of that pain; none closes
it at the intersection of process mining, stochastic forecasting, and
SDLC application.
Table~\ref{tab:related-surveys} summarizes positioning; the paragraphs
below state, for each strand, contribution toward the pain and residual
limitation.

\paragraph{Process mining (algorithms and streaming).}
Augusto et al.~\cite{augusto2019} contribute a comprehensive map of
automated discovery algorithms; Burattin~\cite{burattin2022} formalizes
streaming process mining.
Limitation relative to the pain: neither addresses SDLC-specific case
correlation across heterogeneous tools, nor advances from discovery to
calibrated delivery forecasting on software process logs.

\paragraph{Mining Software Repositories (MSR).}
Hassan~\cite{hassan2008road} and Kagdi, Collard, and
Maletic~\cite{kagdi2007} contribute roadmaps and taxonomies for mining
artifacts (code, commits, bug reports).
Limitation: the focus is predominantly on artifacts rather than on the
process flows that connect them across the lifecycle---so structural
blindness in forecasting is not resolved.

\paragraph{Predictive and prescriptive process monitoring.}
Márquez-Chamorro et al.~\cite{marquezChamorro2018} and Kubrak et
al.~\cite{kubrak2022} contribute maps of predictive and prescriptive
monitoring techniques.
Limitation: surveys treat business processes generically and do not
examine SDLC delivery forecasting under process-aware stochastic models.

\paragraph{Stochastic forecasting for software projects.}
Vacanti~\cite{vacanti2015} contributes a practical throughput-based Monte
Carlo method that answers the delivery question with a distribution, but
without process structure.
Whittaker and Thomason~\cite{whittaker1994} contribute Markov models for
testing.
Limitation: neither connects stochastic methods to
process-mining-discovered SDLC models; the black box remains closed.

\section{Expected Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

Each contribution below is stated together with how it reduces the
operational pain and narrows the research gap of Section~\ref{sec:problem}.

\textbf{C1 (Method):} A formally specified method (PATHCAST) that
integrates process mining, Markov chain estimation, Monte Carlo
simulation, and machine learning into a four-stage PATHCAST pipeline for
probabilistic forecasting of software development processes, with
inter-stage data contracts, formal properties, and correctness guarantees.
\emph{Pain/gap:} attacks technical fragmentation by replacing ad hoc
composition with an explicit L3-oriented method (Chapter~\ref{ch:slr}).

\textbf{C2 (Event Log Construction):} A configurable framework for
constructing structured event logs from heterogeneous software development
process repositories, addressing case correlation through a cascade of
explicit, structural, and heuristic strategies with formal quality
metrics.
\emph{Pain/gap:} makes the digital trace of the real flow usable as
input to forecasting, independently of later stages.

\textbf{C3 (Process-Aware Stochastic Forecasting):} An empirically
validated demonstration that Monte Carlo simulation parameterized by
Markov models estimated from process-mining-discovered models produces
more accurate and better-calibrated forecasts than throughput-based Monte
Carlo for software development processes.
\emph{Pain/gap:} directly reduces structural blindness by opening the
throughput black box with measured state transitions and rework.

\textbf{C4 (Process-Derived Feature Set):} A set of features derived from
process mining and Markov analysis (conformance scores, rework counts,
Markov-expected remaining times) for machine learning-based outcome
prediction, with empirical comparison against traditional MSR features.
\emph{Pain/gap:} tests whether process-derived signals improve on
repository metrics alone (H1), addressing residual predictors beyond the
stochastic stage.

\textbf{C5 (Empirical Evidence):} A reproducible empirical evaluation on
open-source software development process repositories, providing baseline results for
stochastic process forecasting in the SDLC and a reusable dataset.
\emph{Pain/gap:} supplies evidence that the gap is closable in practice
and that the method meets the reproducibility bar demanded of the
reviewed corpus (Chapter~\ref{ch:slr}).

\section{Research Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology-overview}

This research adopts the Design Science Research (DSR) paradigm, following
the framework of Hevner et al.~\cite{hevner2004} and the methodology
of Peffers et al.~\cite{peffers2007}. DSR is the appropriate paradigm
because the primary goal is to design, implement, and evaluate an artifact
(the PATHCAST method and its supporting tool) that addresses a
practical problem identified in the literature.

The DSRM process is instantiated as follows:

\textbf{Activity 1 (Problem Identification):} The research problem is
identified through analysis of the literature at the intersection of
process mining, MSR, and stochastic forecasting, revealing the descriptive
ceiling, structural blindness, and technical fragmentation described in
Section~\ref{sec:problem}.

\textbf{Activity 2 (Objectives):} The objectives are derived from the
identified gaps: a method that advances beyond descriptive analysis,
incorporates process structure into forecasting, and integrates
complementary techniques into a formally specified PATHCAST pipeline.

\textbf{Activity 3 (Design and Development):} The PATHCAST method
is designed as a four-stage pipeline with formal inter-stage contracts,
implemented as a Python-based tool using PM4Py for process mining, NumPy
for Markov computation, and scikit-learn/XGBoost for machine learning.

\textbf{Activity 4 (Demonstration):} The method is demonstrated on
open-source software development process repositories, producing event logs, process models,
Markov estimates, Monte Carlo forecasts, and ML predictions.

\textbf{Activity 5 (Evaluation):} The evaluation is structured into two
complementary stages:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{E1 (Pipeline Validation):} Assesses the quality of event
    log construction (coverage, correlation rate) and process discovery
    (fitness, precision, generalization) across the study repositories.
    Addresses the structural prerequisites of the central research question
    (Table~\ref{tab:rq-obj-map}).

    \item \textbf{E2 (Forecasting Accuracy):} Compares the proposed
    process-aware Monte Carlo against baselines (throughput-based MC,
    analytical Markov, historical mean) using temporal hold-out validation.
    Evaluates ML feature sets through cross-validated comparison.
    Addresses H1--H3 and specific objective~4.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Activity 6 (Communication):} Results are communicated through
this thesis and through submissions to venues in empirical software
engineering and process mining (e.g., EASE, MSR, ICPM).

\section{Structure of the Document}
\label{sec:structure}

The remainder of this document is organized as follows.

Chapter~\ref{ch:theoretical-foundation} presents the theoretical
foundations, covering process mining, Mining Software Repositories (MSR),
Markov chains for software process modeling, Monte Carlo simulation for
process forecasting, and machine learning for software engineering.

Chapter~\ref{ch:slr} presents a systematic literature review following
Kitchenham and Charters' guidelines, mapping the state of the art at the
intersection of process mining, stochastic modeling, and machine learning
applied to software process forecasting, and identifying the research gap
addressed by the proposed method---linked to the operational pain stated
in this chapter.

Chapter~\ref{ch:method} presents the proposed method (PATHCAST),
formalizing the four-stage PATHCAST pipeline with input/output contracts,
algorithms, formal properties, and theoretical guarantees.

Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} details the research methodology, including
the Design Science Research framework, the experimental design for the
two evaluation stages, and threats to validity.

A dedicated implementation chapter (in preparation) will describe the
implementation architecture, technology choices, and design decisions.

The empirical-evaluation chapter (in preparation) will present the
empirical evaluation, including the results of pipeline validation (E1)
and forecasting accuracy assessment (E2), with statistical analysis and
comparison against baselines.

The concluding chapter (in preparation) will discuss the results, identify
limitations, propose directions for future work, and present the
conclusions.
